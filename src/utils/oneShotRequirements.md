## REQUIREMENTS

Generate a one-shot adventure that:

* Can be completed in a single session of 3–6 hours.
* Uses ALL provided tropes naturally.
* Has personal stakes and local consequences, rather than world-ending stakes.
* Continues the supplied history and suits the existing party’s level, abilities and relationships. If party size or level is missing, state a practical provisional assumption.
* Includes meaningful player choices and a satisfying resolution within the session.
* Gives the DM a complete understanding of the scenario, including secrets the players have not discovered.
* Clearly distinguishes objective truth, NPC beliefs, player-facing information and deliberate misinformation.
* Explains the causes, motivations, mechanisms and consequences behind major events. Do not substitute evocative descriptions or unexplained mysteries for information the DM needs.
* Allows players to discover essential information through multiple routes, without requiring one successful check or a predetermined decision.
* Includes a consolidated overview of all key items players may encounter, acquire, seek or hear about, including items that remain inaccessible or in someone else’s possession.
* Keeps the playable scope manageable: use a compact conflict, a limited cast and 3–4 core scenes. Detailed DM reference material should clarify the adventure without adding mandatory scenes or extending it beyond one session.

## OUTPUT STRUCTURE

### 1. Adventure Title and Premise

Provide a title and a 1–2 paragraph summary. Clearly label any player-facing introduction separately from DM-only information.

State the intended party size and level, expected session length, and how the opening follows from the supplied campaign history.

### 2. DM-Only: What Is Actually Going On

This section is mandatory. It contains the full underlying truth, including spoilers. Never withhold information from the DM to preserve a player-facing mystery.

Include:

* The underlying situation: Explain plainly what is really happening, what caused it, why it matters and why the adventure begins now.
* Relevant history and timeline: Describe the events that created the current conflict in chronological order, identifying who did what and why. Distinguish recorded history from newly invented connections.
* The villain’s actual objective and plan: Explain the intended outcome, motivation, operational steps, required resources, current progress, constraints and conditions for success or failure. Explain why the villain has not already succeeded.
* Other hidden agendas: Describe relevant NPC and faction secrets, alliances, betrayals, competing goals and misunderstandings. Clarify what each major actor knows, believes and conceals.
* How important mechanisms work: Explain any rituals, curses, artifacts, prophecies, supernatural forces or other devices central to the plot. Specify their origins where relevant, capabilities, limits, costs, triggers and ways they can be disrupted. Define usable rules for any invented mechanism.
* The party’s involvement: Explain why these characters become involved, why their actions matter and how their established backgrounds, relationships, possessions or goals connect to the conflict. Label invented connections and avoid assuming how the characters will respond.
* Secrets and discoveries: Use a table listing each major hidden truth, the apparent explanation available to players, who knows the truth, and the clues or routes through which it can be discovered. Provide multiple independent routes to essential revelations that fit within the one-shot.
* What happens without intervention: Give the likely sequence of events if the party delays, fails, refuses the quest or walks away. Include local consequences, the relevant timing or triggers, and opportunities to re-engage without forcing participation.
* Possible resolutions: Explain how major player choices can alter the plan, climax and aftermath. Include plausible alternatives to killing the main villain.

Provide enough concrete detail that the DM can explain the scenario in their own words, answer likely player questions and adjudicate unexpected actions without inventing missing foundations. Keep background detail focused on what helps run this adventure.

### 3. Adventure Hooks

Provide 3 hooks drawing the PCs into the quest.

For each, explain:

* The player-facing invitation.
* Its actual connection to the underlying conflict.
* Its relevance to the supplied party or recent campaign events.
* How it leads promptly into the adventure.

Treat these as alternative or complementary entry points, not three mandatory preliminary quests.

### 4. BBEG & Henchmen

Describe the main villain, 1–2 supporters and thematic creatures.

Include their motivations, roles in the plan, relationships, knowledge and likely reactions to interference. Explain what they want from the party, what might persuade them to negotiate or retreat, and any relevant faction connections.

Provide suitable stat-block references or clearly labelled homebrew mechanics, with encounter assumptions appropriate to the party.

### 5. Main Locations

Describe 4 important locations, including:

* Player-facing appearance and atmosphere.
* Their role in the adventure and connection to the underlying conflict.
* Relevant history, threats and occupants.
* DM-only secrets and discoverable clues.
* Relevant key items, with references to Section 7.
* Connections and travel time between locations.

Locations may share a core scene or be optional. Do not require four separate extended encounters merely to use every location.

### 6. Major NPCs

Provide 2–3 supporting NPCs with motives and ties to the conflict.

Explain what each knows, what they want from the party, what they conceal or misunderstand, and how their behaviour could change. Include relevant relationships with existing PCs, the villain or factions, distinguishing supplied facts from invented connections.

Make their practical role clear: for example, witness, ally, suspect, rival or person at risk.

### 7. Key Items: Overview and DM Reference

Provide a consolidated reference for all narratively or mechanically significant items that players may encounter or hear about. Include relevant artifacts, magic items, weapons, relics, keys, documents, ritual components, special materials and personal possessions. Include legendary, lost, destroyed or rumoured items when they matter to the adventure. Routine equipment does not need an entry unless it has a specific story role.

Start with an overview table:

| Item | Description and identifying features | Adventure significance | Current location or holder | How players may encounter it or hear of it |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Follow with a concise reference entry for each key item, covering:

* Player-facing information: What characters can initially observe or reasonably know, including its name, appearance and reputation.
* Rumours and beliefs: What people claim about it, who makes those claims, and whether each claim is true, false or incomplete.
* DM-only truth: Its actual origin, purpose, history, secrets and connection to the underlying conflict. Explain why relevant NPCs or factions want it.
* Actual properties and use: What it does and how it is used. For usable items, specify applicable activation requirements, attunement, charges, duration, limitations, costs, risks and ways to disable or destroy it. Clearly label homebrew rules.
* Discovery and identification: How players can locate it and learn its true nature. State what observation, research, NPC testimony, checks or identification magic can reveal, including any justified limits.
* Availability and timing: Whether the item is obtainable, which scene may introduce it, and any prerequisites for acquiring or using it. Clarify whether it is a potential reward, a temporary resource or background lore.
* Choices and consequences: Explain the relevant effects of taking, using, surrendering, losing, destroying or ignoring it. For plot-critical items, explain how the adventure can continue if the party never obtains them.
* Connections: Reference the relevant NPCs, factions, locations, scenes and revelations.

Use enough detail to make each item understandable and usable without requiring the DM to invent essential properties. Mark irrelevant fields as not applicable rather than adding unnecessary mechanics. Keep powerful obtainable items appropriate to the party’s level, and explain deliberate exceptions and their consequences for the ongoing campaign.

Keep the item list focused on the one-shot. Do not introduce additional fetch quests merely to showcase items.

### 8. Core Scenes

Provide 3–4 major scenes with conflicts and meaningful player choices, building towards a climax that can resolve within the session.

Give an estimated duration for each scene and an overall pacing plan that fits 3–6 hours, including the opening and aftermath. Identify what can be shortened or omitted if time runs low without removing essential clues or making the resolution incoherent.

For each scene or encounter, include:

* Location and participants: Where it occurs and which NPCs or creatures are involved.
* Player-facing situation: What the characters initially perceive.
* DM-only explanation: What is actually happening, why it is happening and how it connects to the wider situation.
* Conflict and stakes: What needs resolving, what creates pressure and what is at risk.
* Discoverable information: Relevant clues, how players can find them and what they reveal. Essential information must not depend on a single successful check.
* Relevant items: Which key items are present, mentioned, sought or usable, with references to Section 7.
* Practical mechanics: Relevant checks, DCs, hazards, combat references or other rules needed to run the scene. Clearly label homebrew mechanics.
* Choices and consequences: Likely outcomes of different approaches, including negotiation, combat, deception, failure, avoidance or unexpected intervention where relevant.
* NPC responses: How relevant actors react and adapt.
* Continuation: How the adventure can proceed from different outcomes without forcing a predetermined choice.

### 9. Rewards & Outcomes

Provide 2–3 distinct resolutions depending on PC choices.

For each, explain:

* What happens to the underlying threat and the villain’s plan.
* The consequences for relevant NPCs, factions and the local area.
* How the party’s choices affect personal stakes and established relationships.
* Available rewards, including relevant key items from Section 7.
* The consequences of failure, compromise or walking away where applicable.
* Any optional threads that can carry into the ongoing campaign.

Resolve the one-shot’s immediate conflict within the session. Future hooks may remain, but must not replace a satisfying conclusion.

### 10. Recurring Themes

Identify 1–2 key themes and explain how they appear through situations, choices and consequences.

Connect them to the selected tropes and party context without prescribing the characters’ feelings or decisions.

### 11. DM Clarity and Consistency Check

Before presenting the final output, review the entire adventure from the perspective of a DM encountering it for the first time. Fix any gaps or contradictions you identify.

Check that:

* The underlying situation and sequence of events are understandable and internally consistent.
* Every major actor has a clear motivation, sufficient knowledge and plausible means to take their described actions.
* The villain’s plan is workable within the established rules, with clear dependencies, limitations and reasons for its timing.
* Important mechanisms are explained well enough to adjudicate at the table.
* Every major mystery has an explicit DM-facing answer.
* Essential revelations have accessible clues and multiple discovery routes within the session.
* NPC behaviour reflects what each NPC actually knows, rather than unexplained knowledge of the plot.
* Hooks, locations, NPCs, scenes and the climax connect coherently.
* Every significant item mentioned elsewhere in the adventure appears in the key items overview, including items known only through rumours or historical accounts.
* Item properties, locations, ownership, histories and availability are consistent throughout.
* The DM can distinguish what players believe about each item from what it actually does.
* Plot-critical item dependencies are explicit, with viable continuations if those items are missed, lost, destroyed or used unexpectedly.
* Player choices meaningfully affect events, and failure or refusal does not leave the DM without a way to continue or conclude the session.
* All provided tropes are integrated, and established lore and campaign continuity are respected.
* Existing PCs retain their supplied identities, histories, abilities, relationships and possessions unless events during play change them.
* Invented connections and provisional assumptions are clearly distinguished from supplied facts.
* The adventure suits the stated party and remains focused on personal stakes and local consequences.
* The scenes, travel, investigation and climax realistically fit 3–6 hours, with a usable shorter-session path.
* The immediate conflict has a satisfying resolution without requiring another session.
* No essential explanation is left as “the DM decides,” “somehow” or an unresolved placeholder.

Conclude with a brief DM Readiness Summary identifying any assumptions, remaining optional decisions, and where the answers to the adventure’s central mysteries and item mechanics are explained. Include the expected session duration and the main pacing adjustment if time runs short.

Do not merely declare that the check passed: provide specific references to the relevant sections. If an essential issue cannot be resolved without user input, identify the exact missing information and provide a clearly labelled provisional solution.
